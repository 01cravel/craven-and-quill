# Craven & Quill story-writing standard

This is the editorial brief for every generated story. Reading level changes how the story is told, never how imaginative it is.

## Rules for every story

- Give the main character a clear want within the first page.
- Build one connected chain of cause and effect across ten spreads.
- Let the character make choices. Do not let coincidence solve the problem.
- Use concrete actions, objects and sensory details instead of explaining feelings.
- Give each speaking character a distinct voice. Keep dialogue short enough to say aloud naturally.
- Include one recurring detail, line or joke, then bring it back at the end.
- Vary sentence length to control pace. Read every page aloud during editing.
- Let the illustration carry visible detail. Do not describe everything shown in the picture.
- End with emotional satisfaction, not a stated lesson.
- Never imitate a living author or a copyrighted character.

## Reading profiles

### Age 3–5

- About 20–40 words per spread.
- Mostly short, direct sentences and familiar words.
- Repetition with a small change each time.
- One simple problem, three attempts and a reassuring ending.
- Sounds, rhyme or a join-in phrase where it fits naturally.

### Age 6–8

- About 45–75 words per spread.
- Familiar sentence structures with occasional new vocabulary made clear by context.
- A mix of short action lines and longer descriptive sentences.
- Natural dialogue on most spreads.
- A clear goal, rising trouble, a reveal and a satisfying callback.

### Age 9–12

- About 80–130 words per spread.
- More varied syntax, precise vocabulary and room for inference.
- A layered problem with meaningful choices and consequences.
- Humour and emotion should come from character, not narrator commentary.

### Teen or adult

- About 110–180 words per spread unless the chosen format calls for less.
- Match the requested genre and tone without making the writing childish.
- Use subtext, sharper character voice and restrained description.
- Avoid neat morals and over-explaining the ending.

## Phrases and habits to reject

Reject generic openings and endings such as “little did they know”, “with one brave step”, “and so the adventure began”, “in that moment”, “filled with wonder” and “they knew they could do anything”. Also reject repeated three-part lists, vague magical glows, unexplained perfection, instant friendship, narrator-written morals and emotion described without visible behaviour.

## Final edit

1. Check that every spread changes the situation.
2. Remove any sentence that could belong to almost any story.
3. Replace vague verbs and adjectives with specific action.
4. Read the whole story aloud for rhythm and awkward phrasing.
5. Check vocabulary and sentence load against the selected reading profile.
6. Confirm that names, appearance, relationships and story facts stay consistent.
7. Confirm that the final page pays off something introduced earlier.
